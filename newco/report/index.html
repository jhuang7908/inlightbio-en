China Biopharma Out-Licensing Deal Survey (2026)

Part 1 | Ten multinational drugmakers paid seven-tenths of upfront cash

China Biopharma Out-Licensing and NewCo Deal Survey Report (2026) — Deal structures, payment realization and value benchmarks

InLight Editorial · November 2026

Original InLight research. Reproduction, excerpting or redistribution requires written permission.

Part 1 of a 12-part series · Current installment

InLight Editorial · November 2026

This InLight survey examines 237 China-origin out-licensing and related deals, supported by company announcements, exchange and SEC filings, and partner releases. Separately, 25 publicly available agreements were coded clause by clause. This 12-part series presents the findings; the full report contains the transaction-level records and sources. [1,12]

1. The headline and the cash are different numbers

China's out-licensing boom makes billion-dollar headlines familiar. DIA Global Forum, citing PharmCube NextBiopharm, reports that annual deal count rose from 94 in 2024 to 157 in 2025, while headline value increased from $51.9bn to approximately $135.7bn. But headline value is not the cash upfront contractually agreed at signing. [13]

In our 237-deal sample, 198 disclosed headline values: the median was $1.1105bn and the mean $1.882bn. Among 172 deals disclosing separable cash upfronts, the median was $55m; the middle half ranged from $20m to $121.25m. [1]

Excluding two all-cash acquisitions—Genmab/ProFoundBio and Gilead/PepStar—leaves 156 deals with comparable upfront-to-headline ratios. Their median was 4.9%, with an interquartile range of 2.6%–7.5%. A nominal "$1bn licence" therefore typically promises about $50m upfront, not $1bn at signing. The pooled ratio is 6.6%; it is a different measure, not an alternative estimate of the deal-level median. [1–3]

2. Record deals lift the mean, not the upfront share

The mean upfront was $153m, 2.79 times the median. The ten largest upfronts account for 40.9% of disclosed cash; remove them and the mean falls to $96.24m. Medians and quartiles better describe an ordinary licensor's position. [1]

Nor did the ratio rise consistently over time. Annual medians were 5.4%, 4.9%, 3.9% and 5.0% for 2023–2026, with ratio samples of 27, 35, 48 and 46. By headline size, medians were 3.7% below $1bn, 5.2% at $1–2bn, 4.7% at $2–5bn and 4.0% at $5bn or above. [1]

3. Six enormous headlines

Six deals announced between July 2025 and October 2026 exceeded $10bn in reported headline value. Together they represent approximately $83.6bn, or 22.4% of the disclosed $372.6bn total. Their combined upfronts were $4.45bn—just 5.3% of their combined headlines. Individual upfronts ranged from $0.4bn to $1.2bn, equivalent to 2.6%–9.6%. [1,4–9]

Those six were Hengrui/GSK, Innovent/Takeda, CSPC/AstraZeneca, Hengrui/Bristol Myers Squibb, Innovent/Pfizer and Hansoh/Roche. All buyers were top-20 pharma companies. Multi-programme packages, including CSPC's eight programmes, Hengrui's HRS-9821 plus eleven GSK options, and Hengrui's thirteen BMS programmes, accumulate distant milestones into impressive headlines. They do not imply equivalent cash per programme. [4–9]

The announced upfront/headline pairs were $0.5bn/$12.5bn for Hengrui/GSK; $1.1bn/$11.4bn for Innovent/Takeda; $1.2bn/$18.5bn for CSPC/AstraZeneca; $0.6bn/$15.2bn for Hengrui/BMS; $0.65bn/$10.5bn for Innovent/Pfizer; and $0.4bn/$15.51bn for Hansoh/Roche. These are announced transaction amounts, not forecasts of milestone collection. At the other extreme, only ten deals had upfront shares above 20%, mostly acquisitions, later-stage or approved assets, and regional licences: buyers were paying for greater certainty. [1,4–9]

4. Who writes the cheques?

The ten buyers ranked by upfront paid 72.3% of all disclosed upfront cash. A separately ranked top ten by headline value accounted for 59.0% of headline value; these are not necessarily the same ten companies. [1]

AstraZeneca leads: 16 deals, approximately $4bn upfront and 15.1% of disclosed cash. Its activity spans CSPC's programmes, the Gracell acquisition and Dizal's sunvozertinib licence. Next come Pfizer, with five deals and approximately $2.1bn, and Merck & Co, with five and approximately $2bn. Roche, including Genentech, has thirteen deals; Genmab enters through one $1.8bn acquisition. Takeda, BMS, Gilead, GSK and Novartis complete the upfront top ten. [1,6,10,11]

BMS's three deals totalled approximately $24.7bn in headline value. Lilly signed ten but paid approximately $0.65bn upfront, outside the top ten. Frequent participation and large cash commitments are different things. [1]

Buyer type matters too. Median upfronts were $113.5m for top-20 pharma, $47m for mid-cap pharma, $20m for biotech and $27.5m for venture-built NewCos. Median upfront shares were 6.8%, 2.4% and 2.7% for top-20 pharma, biotech and NewCos respectively. The overall $55m median hides different bidding populations. [1]

5. Sellers are concentrated too

The ten licensors ranked by upfront collected 53.4% of disclosed cash; a separately ranked top ten by headline held 52.6% of headline value. Innovent led upfronts with five deals and approximately $2.2bn. Hengrui recorded twelve deals and approximately $45.3bn in headlines. Harbour BioMed, including Nona, had thirteen deals, but less than $0.5bn upfront. [1]

Five licensors in the upfront top ten—ProFoundBio, PepStar, 3SBio, Gracell and Regor—each entered through a single deal. Hengrui, CSPC and Hansoh instead appeared through repeated licensing activity. A concentrated seller ranking therefore mixes repeat participants with companies or asset portfolios transformed by one large transaction. It should not be mistaken for a league table of recurring licensing revenues. [1]

6. Why headline values are high and upfronts low

A licence can be understood as the buyer's option: the upfront is the premium, milestones are contingent exercise prices, and termination for convenience permits abandonment. Of 25 public agreements, 21 contained convenience termination, always available only to the licensee. In ten agreements with separable milestone types, sales milestones represented a median 78% of milestone ceilings. [12]

This explains why a larger headline need not increase immediate cash. Contract structure leaves much of the uncertainty contingent on development and commercial success. For licensors, fewer buyers capable of large upfronts also means less scope to negotiate cash forward.

7. Coverage, interpretation and the next question

Our 2025 subset contains 72 deals and approximately $113.9bn of headline value, about 84% of DIA's cited industry total. Primary-source requirements leave smaller deals under-covered; this is not a census. [1,13]

The baseline covers 234 deals to 22 October 2026. Three targeted additions announced on 4 November bring it to 237—not a complete later sweep. Upfronts mean separable contractual cash, not verified receipt. Sino Biopharm's unsplit $358m upfront/near-term package is excluded; Hinova's $20m cash is included and its $10m warrants kept separate. Undisclosed terms are not estimated. [1,14–16]

Evaluate upfronts first, near-term development milestones second, and sales milestones and headlines last. Compare like development stages and territories; retained Greater China rights, regional licences and global licences are not interchangeable.

The practical question is not simply whether a headline is large enough. Ask which development events trigger near-term payments, how much depends on eventual sales, and what termination notice and cost-sharing provisions protect the licensor. Those contract details determine how much of an attractive announcement can ultimately be realised.

Part 2 | A Billion-Dollar License Has an Illustrative Risk-Adjusted Value of About $80 Million

By InLight Editorial

One question: What is a billion-dollar licensing headline worth after adjusting for risk and time?

About $80 million, in the white paper's illustrative Phase I model—not as an observed market average. With a $1 billion headline and a 12% discount rate, the published scenarios produce approximately $73–83 million in risk-adjusted payment value. [1] This distinction belongs beside the headline, not buried in a disclaimer: the result is neither cash already received nor a valuation of any specific asset.

The headline is a ceiling, not a receivable

Licensing announcements combine payments with different conditions. The upfront is treated as a signing-date payment in this simplified model. Development, regulatory and sales milestones depend on events that may happen later—or never. Adding those amounts describes a potential contractual ceiling. It does not show what the licensor owns in cash today.

Risk-adjusted net present value, or rNPV, weights future payments by the probability of reaching their triggering events and discounts them for time. Here the exercise concerns contractual payment streams. It is not a complete asset valuation net of every development obligation, and it excludes unmodeled royalties. [1] Contractual payment terms also do not establish that an upfront has actually arrived.

Separate sample statistics from assumptions

The released white paper covers 237 core transactions and 25 publicly available agreements. Its stage-specific median upfront shares are 3.9% for preclinical/IND deals, 5.4% for Phase I and 6.7% for Phase II, with respective sample sizes of 39, 43 and eight. [1] These are sample descriptions, not mandatory pricing benchmarks. The small Phase II sample is particularly important when interpreting comparisons.

Among the agreements, only ten distinguish development and sales milestones sufficiently for this calculation. Their median sales-milestone share is 78% of the milestone ceiling. The model therefore assigns 78% of the non-upfront amount to sales milestones and 22% to development and regulatory milestones. [1] This is a ceiling composition, not the share of money eventually collected.

Other inputs are assumptions: how development milestones are distributed across stages, sales milestones beginning roughly 2.5 years after approval, conditional sales-trigger scenarios of 25%, 50% and 100%, and a 12% discount rate. [1] Those probabilities are not observed payment frequencies, and the discount rate is not a measurement of each company's financing cost.

Probability and time apply different discounts

The model uses historical BIO/Informa/QLS transition rates: 52.0% from Phase I to II, 28.9% from II to III, 57.8% from III to submission and 90.6% from submission to approval. [2] These are historical, cross-indication averages, not a dedicated estimate for Chinese assets developed overseas or a claim to the latest forecast.

Multiplying the transitions gives approximately 7.9% cumulative approval probability from Phase I in the illustrative framework. At a 12% discount rate, a dollar payable ten years later is worth approximately $0.32 today. [1] Neither calculation means that every individual program has identical prospects. Together, they explain why a large, distant sales commitment can contribute relatively little to current value.

A better clinical package can change both the probability and the timeline. Conversely, a longer development program can delay milestones even without changing their nominal amounts. A credible comparison must make those assumptions visible rather than present a single, apparently exact answer.

The sample medians also do not reconstruct a real "average contract." Agreements differ in indications, territories and milestone definitions. Combining separate median inputs creates an explanatory scenario, not a transaction that necessarily exists in the database. Readers should not use the diagram to infer undisclosed terms for a named company, or assume that the same milestone structure applies across the entire market.

Where "about $80 million" comes from

For the illustrative $1 billion Phase I license, the upfront is $54 million. At conditional sales-trigger probabilities of 25%, 50% and 100%, published risk-adjusted values are approximately $73 million, $76 million and $83 million. The upfront accounts for 65–74% of present value. [1] "About $80 million" summarizes that range; it is not an additional calculated mean.

The same framework produces approximately $63–71 million for preclinical/IND signing and $95–120 million for Phase II. [1] Moving the signing stage changes the answer. The Phase I headline cannot therefore be applied unchanged to a preclinical program, a late-stage asset or an approved medicine.

Even the optimistic sales scenario retains the earlier development and approval risks. "All sales milestones trigger" means all trigger in the post-approval scenario—not that every program reaches approval. In the Phase I illustration, moving from the lowest to highest sales scenario adds only about $10 million to present value. [1] That is a sensitivity result, not proof that commercial milestones are worthless.

External studies are not validation of this model

Recon Strategy estimates, from clinical progress, that Phase I licenses could realize about 32% of potential value after nine years; its preclinical analysis estimates less than 20%. [3] These are modeled progress-based estimates, not audits of transaction-by-transaction cash receipts. Different denominators, observation periods and discounting prevent direct calibration of this rNPV illustration.

Nor should unrelated percentages be assembled into a single payment curve. Asset-specific efficacy, safety, competition, global development plans, rights and cost sharing can materially change economics. A useful model exposes those differences. It does not remove them by applying a universal haircut to every headline.

For a licensor comparing offers, align the development stage, licensed rights and payment definitions before testing assumptions. If a reasonable change in timing or probability reverses the ranking, retain that uncertainty explicitly. Choosing the most favorable scenario and reporting it as the answer would defeat the purpose of risk adjustment. The relevant decision is about conditional cash flows, not which announcement contains the largest number.

The practical reading rule

Start with contractual cash upfront, then examine near-term development milestones, then distant sales milestones. For each payment, check its trigger, timing and responsibilities. A larger headline alone does not establish a better offer. This installment's model is a tool for reading economic conditions, not a substitute for diligence, investment advice or a quotation for a particular asset.
