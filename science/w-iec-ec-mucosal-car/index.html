Mucosal CAR-positive T-cell expansion defines cilta-cel-associated enterocolitis

MD Anderson reports 9 cases of cilta-cel-associated immune effector cell enterocolitis (IEC-EC) with CAR-positive infiltration among 158 cilta-cel-treated patients: overall incidence 5.7% (9 of 158) and 6-month competing-risk incidence 5.1% (CI 1.7–8.6%). Compared with 8 cases of CAR-negative enterocolitis, the CAR-positive group had a median of 48 versus 5 hospital days, a mean of 2.4 versus 0.16 infections, death in 4 of 9 versus 0 of 8 (absolute risk difference 44.4%, CI 5.6–78.4%), and parenteral nutrition in 4 of 9 versus 0 of 8. After day 50, peripheral-blood CAR-T persistence was a median 101.84-fold higher. Mucosal infiltration was duodenum-predominant (estimated 63.5% of CD3+ cells CAR+). The authors describe IEC-EC as a duodenum-predominant clonal lymphoproliferative disorder, distinct from peripheral T-cell lymphoma. CLDN9 findings remain hypothesis-generating.

Background and open questions

IEC-EC after cilta-cel has been reported as severe diarrhea associated with clonal CAR T-cell populations, and it has entered safety-warning discussions. Deciding whether it is nonspecific inflammation, a clonal lymphoproliferative disorder, or something closer to malignant transformation requires looking at mucosal infiltration, T-cell clones and vector-integration sites in the same cases.

This paper, from MD Anderson, used dense CAR-positive infiltration (CAR+ cells ≥20% of CD3+ T cells in at least one biopsy site) as an operational definition and compared those patients with CAR-negative post-cilta-cel enterocolitis (EC-NOS).

Study design

The authors identified 9 patients with dense CAR+ infiltrates among patients with enterocolitis; remaining CAR-negative enterocolitis cases had only scattered CAR+ cells (<10% of CD3+ cells and <1% of all cells). They then used multiplex immunofluorescence, transcriptomics, flow cytometry, T-cell receptor sequencing and vector-integration analysis, and estimated incidence on a denominator of 158 consecutive cilta-cel infusions. Seventy contemporaneous ide-cel recipients at the same center were compared for severe diarrhea requiring extensive hospitalization. Claudin 9 (CLDN9), a previously reported off-target binder of cilta-cel, was quantified as a hypothesis-generating mechanism.

Key results

Clinical contrast and incidence

Compared with EC-NOS, the CAR+ group had a median of 48 versus 5 hospital days, a mean of 2.4 versus 0.16 post-endoscopy infections, death in 4 of 9 versus 0 of 8 (absolute risk difference 44.4%, CI 5.6–78.4%), and parenteral nutrition in 4 of 9 versus 0 of 8. Among 158 consecutive infusions, overall incidence was 5.7% (9 of 158); with progression or death as competing risks, 6-month incidence was 5.1% (CI 1.7–8.6%). Two-year non-relapse mortality in the population was 11.4%; IEC-EC contributed to 4 of 10 non-relapse deaths. In the same period, 0 of 70 ide-cel recipients had severe diarrhea requiring extensive hospitalization (Fisher’s exact P = 0.03). Prespecified baseline characteristics did not differ significantly. Progression-free survival in the whole cohort was 63.4% at a median follow-up of 217 days.

Mucosal sites, phenotype and persistence

Estimated marginal means showed the highest CAR+ fraction among CD3+ cells in the duodenum (63.5%), then terminal ileum 36.4%, colon 14.0%, stomach 6.22%, rectum 3.36% and esophagus 2.11% (duodenum versus other sites: GLMM P = 2×10−8, OR 9.41, CI 4.3–20.58). CAR+ cells were often CD4-biased, with case-to-case variation. Versus adjacent CAR− T cells, PD1 positivity was 51.6% versus 17.9% and GZMB 37.3% versus 20.8%. In the duodenum, GZMB+ T cells were estimated at 62.1% in IEC-EC versus 15.2% in EC-NOS. CD103+ tissue-resident T cells were reduced (18.6% versus 35.3% of CD3+ cells) and inversely correlated with CAR+ infiltration (Spearman ρ = −0.51, P = 0.0015). Ki-67 did not differ.

After day 50, time-matched peripheral-blood CAR T-cell persistence was a median 101.84-fold higher in IEC-EC (the abstract says 100-fold). All cases showed monoclonal or oligoclonal T-cell populations by vector integration, flow cytometry and TCR sequencing. Clonal vector integration occurred in introns of highly expressed genes, without evidence of vector-mediated mutagenesis. Candidate driver mutations were sparse and heterogeneous, which the authors use to distinguish IEC-EC from peripheral T-cell lymphoma after CAR-T therapy.

CLDN9

Little or no BCMA expression was found in the gut in 5 IEC-EC cases. In a duodenum-only analysis, CLDN9-positive epithelial area was estimated at 9.1% in IEC-EC, 1.3% in EC-NOS and 1.1% in untreated controls (about 7.38-fold). A pooled gastrointestinal analysis was not significant, and non-duodenal tissues had higher CLDN9 expression, which does not by itself explain duodenal localization. The authors call this finding hypothesis-generating.

Mechanistic interpretation

What the paper shows: In this center’s consecutive population, IEC-EC with dense CAR+ infiltration occurred in about 5–6% of recipients and had a distinctly worse course than CAR-negative enterocolitis; infiltration was duodenum-predominant, and CAR+ cells were cytotoxic and PD1-high; onset was linked to high CAR-T persistence after day 50; clonal evidence supports a gut-tropic lymphoproliferative process whose molecular features differ from peripheral T-cell lymphoma.

What still needs testing: Whether CLDN9 as an off-target antigen drives duodenal localization remains a hypothesis; whether preceding gastrointestinal infection helps initiate the clone is discussed, not proven; nine single-center cases cannot define a general incidence or a standard therapy.

Limitations and uncertainties

  • Single-center series with few cases (9 CAR+, 8 CAR− controls) and wide confidence intervals.
  • The ≥20% operational threshold aids grouping but may exclude borderline cases.
  • The ide-cel comparison is a retrospective exact test at one center, not a head-to-head trial.
  • CLDN9 results are limited to a duodenal subgroup and are labeled hypothesis-generating by the authors.
  • Vector integration should not be equated with malignant transformation; IEC-EC should not be written up as secondary lymphoma on the basis of this paper.

Clinical and industry implications

Read with the same-day multicenter clinical series, IEC-EC looks like a delayed, clonal mucosal toxicity that needs active recognition: long hospitalizations and nutritional failure can follow endoscopy, and high CAR-T persistence after day 50 may be a risk signal, though no screening threshold has been set. The absence of equivalently severe diarrhea among contemporaneous ide-cel recipients at this center hints at product differences, which still need confirmation elsewhere. Mechanistically, IEC-EC should not be conflated with peripheral T-cell lymphoma, and CLDN9 should not be described as a proven pathogenic target.
